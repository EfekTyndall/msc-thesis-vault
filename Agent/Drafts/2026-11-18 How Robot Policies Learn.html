How robot policies learn from demonstrations

A refresher on the building blocks: what a policy is, what data trains it, how VR teleop and handheld (UMI-style) data reach training, what absolute, delta and relative actions mean, and how the robot turns a prediction into motion in its own frame.

1 · The policy

A policy is a function: what the robot sees → what the gripper should do next

Policy = a neural network that maps an observation to actions. It runs in a loop on the robot computer, several times per second.

Observation = what goes in: camera images, the gripper width, often the gripper pose (called state or proprioception), and for the large models a text instruction.

Action chunk = what comes out: not one command, but the next ~1 s of gripper poses plus gripper width. The robot executes part of it, then asks the policy again.

Model families: Diffusion Policy (used by UMI, chi2024), and the large vision-language-action models (VLA) π0.5 and GR00T N1.7 used in this thesis. A VLA is a pretrained model that already knows images and language; it is fine-tuned (trained further) on the thesis demonstrations.

How training works: imitation

Imitation learning (also called behaviour cloning) = the policy is shown many demonstrations and learns to output what the human did. For each time step in each demo, the network gets the observation and is corrected towards the action that actually followed. After many passes over the data, it produces similar actions for similar observations, also in situations it has not seen exactly.

The policy never learns joint angles or "how a UR10e works". It learns where the gripper should go next, given what the camera sees. How to drive the joints there is solved separately by the robot (section 6).

A dataset is a stack of episodes, each a timeline at 30 Hz

3 · Two ways to collect the same kind of data

VR teleop moves the robot; the handheld moves only the human

VR teleoperation

Operatormoves the PICO controller, grip button held
Headsetcontroller pose in the room frame, 90 Hz
Clutch mappingchange since press × scale, axis swap
UR10eIK → joints; moves the real gripper
Recorderrobot camera, joint states, gripper pose in base frame, width

Everything stored comes from the robot itself, so it is already in the robot base frame. Joint states are recorded too, but the thesis trains on gripper poses (D07), the same as the handheld.

Handheld (UMI-style)

Operatorholds the Dex1-1 handheld with the controller as grip
Headsetcontroller pose in the room frame, 90 Hz
Offsetcontroller → gripper tip (D09 hand-eye)
Recorderwrist camera, gripper-tip pose in room frame, width
Exportabsolute; training makes it relative

No robot is involved, so there are no joint states. The poses are in the headset's room frame. Section 4 and 5 show why that is fine with relative actions.

Both paths produce the same episode rows: image + gripper pose + width. That is what makes the comparison possible. The image must also match: the same camera at the same place relative to the fingers, which is why UMI insists on an identical gripper and camera on the handheld and the robot (chi2024).

4 · Frames

Where the numbers are measured from

Frame = a reference point plus three axes. A pose (position + orientation) only means something together with its frame.

  • Room frame (headset): set when the XRoboToolkit app starts. Origin = headset position at that moment, up = against gravity, forward = headset heading (zhao2025; OpenXR LOCAL space). Holding the Home button or restarting the app makes a new one. The headset's cameras keep it fixed to the room while the headset moves, so a worn, neck-hung or fixed headset gives poses in the same kind of frame.
  • Robot base frame: fixed to the UR10e's foot. The robot always knows its gripper pose in this frame from its joint encoders (forward kinematics).
  • Gripper frame: attached to the gripper tip and moves with it. "Forward" here means "where the fingers point".

The handheld records in the room frame, the robot works in the base frame. The demo below shows how relative actions bridge that gap without calibrating one frame to the other.

5 · Action types

Absolute, delta and relative: the same motion, three ways of writing it

Absolute

Each future pose as coordinates in a fixed frame.

target_k = T_k

Depends on where the frame is. Needs the room → base link.

Delta

Each step as the change from the step before.

Δ_k = T_k − T_(k−1)

Small numbers, but errors add up over steps (UMI, chi2024).

Relative

Each future pose seen from the gripper's current pose.

rel_k = T_0⁻¹ · T_k

Frame cancels out. Used by UMI, ViTaMIn-B, HiFi-UMI, GR00T N1.7.

Names differ between papers and code. In openpi, "delta actions" means action − state per number, i.e. relative to the chunk start but still along the frame's axes. GR00T N1.7 calls the gripper-frame version "RELATIVE" and has DELTA and ABSOLUTE as separate options.

Absolute numbers change with every frame; delta numbers change when the frame is rotated; relative numbers never change. That is why relative actions need no room → base calibration. Turn on "Replay on robot" to see the same relative actions applied from a different robot start pose.

6 · Deployment

How the robot moves the gripper in its own frame

The key: the robot always knows its own current gripper pose in the base frame, from its joint encoders. The policy only says how to move from there. Click through the loop.

1 Observewrist image + width
2 Predictpolicy → relative chunk
3 Anchorcurrent pose from encoders
4 Targetcurrent × relative
5 MoveIK → joints → servo

Forward kinematics (FK) = joint angles → gripper pose. Inverse kinematics (IK) = gripper pose → joint angles. Both are pure geometry of the arm; the UR10e controller does both, so neither has to be learned. The XRoboToolkit teleop examples use the same pattern for teleop: target = start pose + controller change, then IK (zhao2025).

7 · Visual servoing

Yes, it is related: both close the loop through the camera

Visual servoing (classic)Learned policy (this thesis)
LoopCamera → error → motion, repeatedCamera → policy → motion chunk, repeated
CameraOften on the gripper ("eye-in-hand")Wrist camera on the Dex1-1 (eye-in-hand)
GoalGiven explicitly: target image features or target poseImplicit: learned from demonstrations and the instruction
RuleHand-designed control law from image error to gripper velocityNeural network trained by imitation
OutputGripper velocity, in the gripper or camera frameFuture gripper poses, relative to the current pose

Both work in the gripper's own frame from what the wrist camera sees, which is why neither needs to know where the room is. A relative-action policy can be seen as a learned, multi-step visual servo. Classic reference: Chaumette and Hutchinson, "Visual servo control, Part I", IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine 2006 (not in bib).

8 · In this thesis

What was decided and what still has to match

  • Decided (D07, 18 Nov): relative gripper actions + absolute gripper width; the pose input to the model is relative or left out. GR00T N1.7 has an exclude_state setting for that; for π0.5 an own input transform is needed.
  • Needed anyway (D09): the controller → gripper-tip offset, from a hand-eye calibration on the UR10e (about 10 poses, ViTaMIn-B method, li2025). Without it the recorded motion is the controller's, not the fingers'.
  • Not needed for training: a room → robot-base calibration per session. It is still used once for the accuracy test of the PICO tracking (D04).
  • Must match: camera and its position relative to the fingers on handheld and robot; same action form for handheld and teleop data.
  • Handheld-only risks: tracking errors go straight into the data (controller out of camera view drifts, no "lost" flag in the stream); a human can make moves the UR10e cannot reach.
