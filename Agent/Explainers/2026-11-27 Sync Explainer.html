Sync Explainer

What the team's diag tool does, how UMI handles it, and how the thesis will do it. Every chart marked real data is drawn from files on the Mac. Marked simulated means the curve was made up on purpose to have a known answer.

Plain version

The question: two things that should happen at the same moment (the hand moves, the camera sees it) are recorded a little apart. How big is that gap, and how do we remove it?

The answer, used by all three: record the same movement twice, once on each stream. Slide one curve sideways until it lies on top of the other. The slide is the delay. They differ only in which two curves they compare:

Team (diag tool)UMIThesis (board wave)
Compareswhat the operator's hand commanded vs. where the robot arm actually isthree separate checks: camera vs. a clock on a screen, robot vs. ping time, gripper vs. a sine commandwhere the controller says it is vs. where the camera sees the board it is taped to

What is a "slide"? Moving one curve sideways in time. The code calls it a lag or shift. The first curve below is the controller. The second is the same motion seen a little later by the camera. Slide the second one back by the right amount and the two lie on top of each other. That amount is the delay.

"Delay" below always means: how many milliseconds late is stream B compared with stream A.

1 · What the team's diag tool does

The team's VR-teleop program (xr_teleoperate, folder teleop/diag/, written by the supervisor, commits d7ad3e4 and 676202a, Aug–Sep 2026) can write a log while a person steers the robot. The first reading was right, with one detail added by reading the code: the two curves are the wrist target after the operator's hand mapping and where the arm really is (computed from its joint angles).

Terms: IK (inverse kinematics) turns a wanted hand position into joint angles. FK (forward kinematics) does the reverse: from measured joint angles it computes where the wrist is. Tick = one pass of the control loop (30 or 50 Hz in the team's code, so 33 or 20 ms).

Where does 8 ms come from? From the robot log, not from the team's tool. Our replay program writes one row every 8 ms (125 rows per second, measured: median step 0.008 s in 3657 rows). A tick is one row. The team's loop runs at 30 Hz, so one team tick is about 33 ms, roughly four of our rows. The "≈31 Hz" button keeps every 4th row to imitate that. The team method moves one tick at a time, so with 33 ms ticks it can only answer in multiples of 33 ms.

What this shows: the robot is ≈120 ms behind its command. The mismatch (mean gap between the curves) falls from 0.93 mm at no slide to 0.13 mm at 16 ticks (128 ms). Two limits of the team's method appear on this real file. It can only answer in whole ticks, so at 30 Hz the answer comes in steps of 33 ms (here 96 ms vs. 121 ms from the finer method). It also gave 104 ms at 125 Hz, a bit under the finer 121 ms. Cause not tested; the plain dot product sums over fewer samples as the slide grows (reasoned only). For the team's purpose (is the stutter 30 ms or 300 ms?) that is accurate enough.

Why the team does not need synchronised clocks, and we nearly do

The team's two curves come from one program on one computer. Our recorder will collect camera and PICO controller data, which come from two devices. In a real desk log of 25 Nov the headset's own timestamp is 579.25 s away from the recording PC's clock (constant within 17 ms over 30 s). So the headset time cannot be compared with the PC time directly; the recorder keeps the PC arrival time and measures the remaining delay with a movement, exactly as in section 3.

2 · How UMI does it

UMI (Chi et al. 2024, chi2024 in the bib) is the handheld gripper the thesis builds on. Its recording has the same problem: the GoPro image, the pose and the gripper width each arrive late by a different amount. It measures each one separately, then fixes the data in two steps. Details are from the vault note "RL5 sync test findings", read verbatim from the paper on 23 Nov; not re-read today.

Camera

A screen shows a QR code containing the screen's own clock time. The camera films it. Delay = time the frame arrived − time shown in the QR code − the screen's own lag.

Needs the screen and the recorder on one clock. Screen lag value is "known" in the paper, not given there.

Robot position

Use the robot's own timestamps if it has them. If not, assume the delay is half of a network ping time (ICMP round trip).

Gripper width

Command the gripper to open and close as a sine wave. Compare the commanded width with the measured width. The slide that makes them overlap is the delay. This is the same slide-and-score idea as the team's, here with a sine so the match has one clear peak.

UMI's own code for this real code

UMI publishes the function that turns two curves into a delay: get_latency in umi/common/latency_util.py (github.com/real-stanford/universal_manipulation_interface, file read on 27 Nov, not in bib). The core is 15 lines. Line numbers are the file's own.

 23 n_samples = int((t_end - t_start) / resample_dt)       # resample_dt = 1/1000: a 1 ms grid
 25 target_samples = regular_sample(x_target, t_target, t_samples)   # straight-line fill-in (interp1d)
 26 actual_samples = regular_sample(x_actual, t_actual, t_samples)
 28 # normalize samples to zero mean unit std
 29 mean = np.mean(np.concatenate([target_samples, actual_samples]))
 30 std = np.std(np.concatenate([target_samples, actual_samples]))
 31 target_samples = (target_samples - mean) / std      # centered and scaled
 32 actual_samples = (actual_samples - mean) / std
 35 correlation = ss.correlate(actual_samples, target_samples)   # score for every possible slide
 36 lags = ss.correlation_lags(len(actual_samples), len(target_samples))
 37 t_lags = lags * resample_dt                         # slide in seconds
 43 latency = t_lags[np.argmax(correlation)]            # the best slide = the latency

So UMI's method has the same five steps as the picture in section 3: read both curves onto a 1 ms grid by straight lines, center and scale, score every slide, take the best. UMI's repo also holds typical numbers in example/eval_robots_config.yaml: robot observation latency 0.0001 s, gripper observation latency 0.01 s, and action latency 0.1 s for both. The file does not say how they were obtained.

Why a screen test has its own error

A 60 Hz screen redraws every 16.7 ms, and the panel adds some lag of its own. So the time the screen "shows" can be wrong by up to about one refresh plus that lag. UMI subtracts a "known" screen lag (l_display); the paper does not give the value. ViTaMIn-B subtracts nothing. The board wave and the robot sine run use no screen, so this error does not appear there. The screen test measures only the camera. It says nothing about the controller.

Then fix the data (worked example, invented numbers)

Rule: compare "taken" times with "taken" times. A "taken" time is the arrival time minus that stream's measured delay. The camera is the slowest stream, so everything is aligned to its frame times.

ItemArrived atOwn delayTaken at
Camera frame10.000 s0.140 s9.860 s
Pose A (x = 100 mm)9.864 s0.010 s9.854 s
Pose B (x = 107 mm)9.878 s0.010 s9.868 s

Interpolation means finding a value between two measured points on a straight line. The frame was taken at 9.860 s, between pose A (9.854 s) and pose B (9.868 s). It is 6 ms after A out of a 14 ms gap, so the pose is 6/14 of the way from 100 to 107: 100 + (6/14) × 7 = 103 mm. Without the correction the frame would be paired with the pose that arrived at the same moment, about 0.13 s later.

Does it matter? UMI's tossing task: 0.875 success with latency matching vs. 0.575 without (Fig. 8). ViTaMIn-B (li2025): final-step success 0.2 without vs. 0.7 with time alignment. Both numbers as recorded in the vault note.

FastUMI (zhaxizhuoma2025) does not measure anything. All streams run on one ROS clock at 60 fps, are cut to 20 Hz, and each image gets the nearest pose in time. That is simplest, but a constant delay of the camera stays hidden inside the data.

3 · How the thesis does it: the board wave

The thesis recorder (software/recorder in dex1-1-handheld, merged in PR 24) writes camera frames and controller poses to one raw file, each with the PC's arrival time. The delay between the two is measured once with a short test, the board wave: tape the PICO controller to a printed marker board, wave it in front of the D435i camera for about 2 minutes. The camera finds the board's position in each image; the controller reports its own position. delay_xcorr.py slides one curve over the other. The robot sine run (R5) would do the same with the robot as mover.

The script handles the two difficulties of our setup: different rates (camera 30 Hz, controller about 83 Hz in our logs, not 90) are both resampled onto a 1 ms grid, and the best slide is refined between neighbours with a parabola, so the answer is finer than the grid. Self-test of the script on simulated data: 40 / 0 / 123 / −17 ms found within 0.15 ms.

How delay_xcorr.py works, picture by picture

The script is the thesis's own (software/recorder/delay_xcorr.py). It does the same five steps as UMI's get_latency (see section 2), with a few changes listed in the table there. It does five things. Press 1 to 5. The curves are a real controller log and a simulated camera that is 55 ms late (noise 0.5 mm, shown in pixels).

Checks and limits in the code: the search range is ±0.3 s in 1 ms steps (601 slides); the script stops with an error if the trimmed overlap is under 4 × 0.3 s = 1.2 s (no reason for the factor 4 is given in the code; it is only a minimum, and a real wave needs many periods); a positive result means the second stream is late; the search range must stay below half the motion period; the two curves must move in the same direction.

How much error is a delay? real speed

Error = hand speed × delay. In the 23 Nov desk log the hand moved at a median 0.04 m/s and 0.15 m/s at the 90th percentile. That is slow desk waving, not a pick-and-place task. The earlier explainer's 0.3 m/s is an assumption and still unverified for the task.

The robot sine run (R5), with the camera on the flange

The camera is on the flange, so it moves with the robot. A marker is fixed on the table. The controller is also fixed to the flange. The robot moves slowly along one axis in a sine (for example ±40 mm at 0.5 Hz). The camera sees the marker slide in the image, the robot log gives the arm's position, and the controller log gives the controller's position. delay_xcorr.py slides the camera curve against the robot curve, then the controller curve against the robot curve.

No spatial calibration is needed for this. Each curve is scaled to the same spread, so pixels versus millimetres and the camera-to-controller distance do not matter. Only the shape over time counts. (The controller-to-tip offset, D09 (calibration), is a separate later step.) One sign detail: the marker moves in the image opposite to the robot, so one curve must be flipped before the comparison, otherwise the best match comes out wrong. The plan has not stated this before. Not yet run.

Where the camera plugs in

For the board wave and the 10-minute take, the D435i has to be on the station Jetson (USB), because the recorder runs there and reads it as a plain webcam. It stays fixed on the desk; only the board moves. Plug it back into the station Pi before leaving the lab.

The open choice

Board wave (recommended)

Done at the desk in minutes, no robot, no safety setup, can be repeated as often as needed. The mover is a hand, so the motion is irregular; use a smooth side-to-side wave so there is a clear peak.

Robot sine run (R5)

Perfectly repeatable motion and the robot's own timestamp as the reference. Needs the robot in remote mode, a person at the stop button, and the controller gripped safely by the 2F-85 (not yet checked).

Both use the same script. The thesis plans an additional marker on the table at the next ordinary replay to get the camera-to-robot delay as well.
